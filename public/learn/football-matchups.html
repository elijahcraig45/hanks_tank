Does "they can't stop the run" predict football games?

We tested whether splitting team strength into run and pass units, and letting a run-heavy offense gain extra against a weak run defense, predicts NFL and FBS games better than a single margin rating. It does not. Here is how the test worked, what it found, and how to defend that answer.

The verdict. measured On the same games and folds as the baseline, none of the matchup models beat the plain margin ridge by a margin that clears noise. The biggest gain was 0.0034 log loss, on the CFB 2025 holdout, and its 95% CI crosses zero. The run/pass split on its own did worse than one EPA number: splitting adds estimation noise and no new signal. At the unit level, the "run-heavy team gains extra against a bad run defense" term is ruled out at about its full predicted size in both sports. The NFL coefficient even has the wrong sign.

Injuries. measured When the opening-day starting RB is out, the NFL team loses 0.4 points per game (CI −1.6 to +0.8), which can't be told apart from zero. When the opening-day starting QB is out, the team loses 2.0 points (CI −3.9 to −0.2).

Background you may want first: ML_COURSE.html covers logistic regression, L1/L2 regularisation, what C does, calibration and proper losses. ML_LESSONS.html covers statistical power, walk-forward splits, and how a false positive gets found and killed. This page does not repeat that material. It applies it to one question.

1. The question, stated so it can be tested

A fan's model of football says: Georgia runs the ball a lot, Team A can't stop the run, so Georgia should win by more than its overall rating suggests. That sentence contains three separable claims:

  1. Components. A team's strength is better described as four numbers (rush offense, pass offense, rush defense, pass defense) than as one.
  2. Additive matchups. Georgia's expected rushing output is Georgia's rush offense plus Team A's rush-defense weakness, and the same for passing.
  3. Interaction. On top of that, the rush matchup should count more for a team that runs more. The weakness is exploited in proportion to how often you attack it.

Claims 1–2 are model B below. Claim 3 is model C. A is the baseline: one margin rating per team. D stacks the unit models on top of A.

2. Opponent adjustment: ridge with team and opponent effects

Raw stats lie about schedule. A rush defense that faced three bad rushing teams looks elite. The fix is to fit every game at once. For each team-game where offense i faced defense j:

rush_EPA_per_play(i vs j) = league_mean + O_rush[i] + D_rush[j] + home_edge × side + noise

One row per team-game, one column per offense and one per defense. The design matrix has a 1 in column O[i] and a 1 in column D[j]. Least squares then solves for everyone's rating at the same time, so beating up weak defenses earns less credit because those defenses' D values are already high (bad). The same structure, with margin as the target and "+1 home, −1 away" columns, is the baseline A.

Why ridge? With about 17 NFL games or 12 FBS games per team per season, 64 rating columns for the NFL and about 260 for CFB are poorly determined. Ridge adds a penalty α·Σrating², which pulls every rating toward zero (league average) unless the data pushes back. That is the regression form of empirical-Bayes shrinkage. Each fit uses only games strictly before the week being predicted, from a two-season window, and down-weights old games by exp(−Δweeks/τ). Plays are the sample weights, so a 70-play game counts more than a 40-play game.

measured The tuning picked a heavier penalty for the unit ratings than for the scalar rating: α=10 for NFL rush and pass versus α=3 for total EPA/play. Per-play rushing EPA is noisy, so the data asked for more shrinkage.

3. Additive versus interaction: the worked example

Team A can't stop the run. Say its rush defense is two standard deviations below average and its pass defense is average. Georgia's offense is average in both units, but it runs more than most teams.

  • Additive model (B). Georgia's expected rush EPA/play goes up by A's weakness, D_rush[A]. Georgia gains that on every run it calls. The additive model already credits "they can't stop the run" for however many runs a typical team calls.
  • Interaction (C). The extra claim is only about Georgia's extra runs above the league mix: (run rate − league run rate) × (D_rush[A] − D_pass[A]) per play. That is a product of two deviations, and both are small.

Defaults: NFL run rate +2 SD (0.066) against a defense that is 2 SD worse against the run (0.06). Points ≈ EPA, which is roughly right in both sports.

measured Across real matchups, the style term has an SD of 0.0015 EPA/play in the NFL (about 0.1 point per game) and 0.0037 in CFB (about 0.24 point). Even if the hypothesis were exactly true, the typical game would move by a tenth to a quarter of a point. A full 2 SD × 2 SD mismatch, which is rare, gets you about 0.25 points in the NFL.

4. Why interactions need far more data

The standard error of a regression coefficient scales as σ/(SD(x)·√n). For a product term x = a·b of two independent, centred quantities, SD(x) = SD(a)·SD(b). Both factors here are small (0.033 × ~0.045 in the NFL), so the product has tiny spread, and each factor is itself an estimate carrying noise. The noise multiplies too. The result is a coefficient that is hard to pin down, estimated from inputs that are partly noise.

measured In the unit-level test we regressed each offense's actual game EPA/play, minus the additive expectation, on the style term. If mix weighting were exactly right, the coefficient would be 1.

5. Regularisation, and the trap it set

Stage 2 is a logistic regression on standardised features, trained only on earlier seasons. We ran the interaction models at C=1 (light penalty) and at C=0.05 (heavy). measured Heavy regularisation made things worse (for example D3 on CFB 2023–24: +0.0057, CI +0.0035 to +0.0077). The reason is that L2 shrinks every coefficient, including the one on the ridge margin that carries nearly all of the signal. The right tool for "trust the base rating, be sceptical of the add-ons" is a differential penalty: leave the base unpenalised, or offset it, and penalise only the new terms. With C=1 the add-on coefficients came out near zero anyway, which is the data's own answer. See the regularisation section of ML_COURSE.html.

6. The scoreboard: walk-forward log loss with paired bootstrap

Every prediction uses only information from before that week. Ratings are refit every (season, week) block. Stage-2 models are trained on earlier seasons only. Ridge hyperparameters were tuned on 2010–16 (NFL) and 2022 FBS (CFB), before any test season. The folds and games are identical to the baseline script, which reproduced its published numbers exactly: 0.6342/0.6339 for the NFL and 0.4948/0.4801 for CFB.

Paired bootstrap. For every game, compute that game's log loss under model X minus under A. Resample the games 2,000 times and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the mean difference. Pairing cancels the game-to-game difficulty that both models share, which is why these CIs are much narrower than the CIs on each log loss separately. Negative is better than A.

How to read it:

  1. Unit ratings alone lose to the margin ridge, badly in CFB. Per-play efficiency leaves out pace, turnover luck, special teams and finishing drives, and the final score captures all of those.
  2. The split is worse than the scalar (B vs B0) everywhere. Four noisy ratings are worse than one pooled rating.
  3. Stacking one EPA rating on top of the ridge (D0) is the best add-on, at −0.002 to −0.003 in CFB, but never significant. The split and the interactions don't improve on D0.

7. Injuries: replacement-level thinking

A player's value is not how good he is. It is how much worse the team gets with his replacement, the backup who actually plays. For a running back, the backup gets roughly the same blocking and the same play calls. For a quarterback, every pass runs through the replacement.

Method (NFL 2008–25, regular season, final week dropped because starters rest). For each team-season, find the starter and compare the games he missed with the games he played, within the same team-season, so team quality cancels. Each game's stats are opponent-adjusted using the opponent's leave-one-out season average. The CIs come from bootstrapping team-seasons.

The definitional trap. measured The first definition, "the season's carries leader," made the RB look worth 1.9 points. That definition is chosen using the outcome. A back piles up carries in games his team leads, and the "leader" of a team whose starter got hurt is often the backup. Defining the starter before the season (the most carries in the team's first four games) and requiring an injury-report listing collapsed the effect to 0.4 points. The QB number went the other way and rose to 2.0, because the leader definition had been labelling backups as starters.

inferred The 2.0-point QB figure averages over every starter who missed time, and most of them are ordinary. An elite QB is plausibly worth considerably more. We didn't split by quality because the samples are too small. The RB result matches the public consensus that running backs are the most replaceable skill position.

CFB. measured Nothing in the current stack carries college injury data. ESPN's free endpoints and the CFBD tiers we probed don't include it. inferred CFBD offers no injury endpoint that we know of, so absences could only be inferred from per-game player usage, with the same selection traps shown above and far more team-seasons with thin samples. We didn't guess any current-week injury or schedule.

8. The limits of what this data can show

  • Resolution. Paired CIs are about ±0.003–0.006 log loss. An effect of 0.1–0.25 points per game, which is how big the style term is, is far below what 2,000–3,000 games can detect in log loss. "Not detected" means "if it exists it is too small to matter at this sample size." It does not mean "proven zero."
  • The unit-level test is stronger because it uses about 5,000 team-games with a continuous target. It rules out the full mix-weighting effect. It does not rule out a small fraction of it.
  • Game script. The NFL run rate is measured in neutral script. CFB's is raw run share, because the CFBD game-level data has no win probability, so CFB run rates partly reflect leading rather than style.
  • Coaching adaptation. Teams game-plan against weaknesses, and opponents adjust. The market and coaches may already arbitrage the effect away inside the game itself.
  • Injury confounding. Absences cluster with other injuries and with late-season context. Within-team-season comparison controls for team quality, not for everything else.
  • CFB sample. Three test seasons, and a 2021 season whose CFBD data covers FBS-involved games only.

9. How to defend this

"Everyone knows matchups matter. Your model is just too crude."

Matchups matter additively, and the ratings already capture that: a bad run defense raises every opponent's expected rushing output. The specific claim that fails is the extra, multiplicative part. Its whole plausible size is 0.1–0.25 points per game, and when we measured it directly on about 5,000 team-games, the full-size version was rejected in both sports.

"Maybe you just used the wrong stat. Try yards per carry."

EPA/play (PPA in CFB) is a better unit measure than yards per carry, because it accounts for down, distance and field position. If the split fails with the better stat, a noisier stat won't rescue it. Also, the split lost to a single pooled EPA number, so the failure comes from adding parameters, not from the choice of stat.

"You tuned it badly. Regularise harder."

We tried. Heavy L2 made things worse, because it also shrinks the base rating. At light penalty the fitted add-on coefficients were already near zero. The unit ratings' own α and τ were tuned on pre-test seasons by next-week prediction error.

"CFB 2025 shows D0 improving 0.003. Isn't that a win?"

Its 95% CI crosses zero, and the 2023–24 walk-forward gain is smaller. By the stress-test rule in ML_LESSONS.html, that is a candidate to watch, not a result. It is also the scalar EPA add-on, not the matchup split.

"Georgia's RB is out. The model should drop them."

By about half a point, going by the NFL evidence, and the CI includes zero. For a QB, about 2 points on average. We have no college injury feed, so any CFB adjustment would be a hand-entered prior, not a measured one.

"How do I know there's no leakage?"

Ratings are refit each week from strictly earlier games. Stage-2 models train only on earlier seasons, and hyperparameters were set before the first test season. The baseline reproduced the earlier published numbers to four decimals on the same games.

10. Recommendation

Don't add matchup (run/pass) modelling to production. It adds complexity and parameters and costs log loss in every fold.

Optional, low priority: a pooled per-play EPA rating stacked onto the margin ridge (D0). Promote it only if it clears zero on the 2026 walk-forward as well.

Injuries: an NFL QB-out adjustment of about −2 points is supportable. Skip it for RBs. Skip CFB entirely until an injury feed exists.

Glossary

EPA / PPA
Expected points added: the change in expected points from before a play to after it. CFBD calls it PPA, predicted points added.
Unit rating
An opponent-adjusted rating for one phase: rush offense, pass offense, rush defense or pass defense.
Additive model
Expected output = offense strength + defense weakness. Effects sum and don't depend on each other.
Interaction
A term whose effect depends on another variable, fitted as a product. Here: run-rate deviation × (run-defense minus pass-defense weakness).
Ridge regression
Least squares plus a penalty α·Σβ² that shrinks coefficients toward zero. Larger α means more shrinkage.
τ (tau)
The decay time for down-weighting old games, weight = exp(−Δweeks/τ).
Walk-forward
Evaluate each period using only models fit on data from before it, the way the model would actually be used.
Log loss
The mean of −log(probability assigned to what happened). A proper score that rewards calibrated confidence. 0.693 is a coin flip.
Paired bootstrap
Resampling games to get a CI on the difference in loss between two models scored on the same games.
Replacement level
The production the team gets from whoever actually replaces the player. Value = starter − replacement, not starter − zero.
Neutral script
Plays where the game state doesn't force run or pass (here: win probability 20–80%, quarters 1–3, downs 1–2).
Selection on the outcome
Defining a group using information that is correlated with the result you then measure, like "season carries leader."
