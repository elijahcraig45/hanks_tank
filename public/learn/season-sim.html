Simulating the rest of the football season, 10,000 times

Games already played stay fixed. Every remaining game is simulated from our best-measured game model, then standings, tiebreakers, conference title games and playoff brackets are worked out in each simulated season. Counting how often something happens gives a probability. This page is about whether those probabilities are honest: we re-ran the simulator from week 3, 6 and 9 of past seasons and scored it against what actually happened.

Short version. The simulator clearly beats naive baselines (current-record extrapolation and coin flips) on every season-level question we scored. Drawing each team's rating from its uncertainty is what makes the probabilities calibrated; without it, 85–95% playoff odds came true only 77% of the time. Letting ratings "react" to simulated results adds nothing once ratings are drawn, and doing both makes the odds too wide. It still loses clearly to a simulator fed the betting market's closing lines (which is an unfair, look-ahead comparison). Treat every number as a probability with real spread, not a call.

How it works

1. A rating for every team

The game model is the margin ridge: each team gets one number (points better than an average team), fitted to scoring margins from the last two seasons with older games weighted down. Home field is one more number; college adds an FBS-versus-FCS term. It is the best winner model we have measured (NFL 2017–24 log loss 0.634 vs 0.646 for our production XGBoost; college 2025 0.480 vs 0.531). A remaining game's margin is drawn from a normal curve centred on the rating difference plus home field, with the spread (σ = 12.5 points NFL, 15.6 college) fitted so single-game win probabilities are calibrated.

2. Rating uncertainty, once per simulated season

After three games nobody knows exactly how good a team is. The ridge is secretly a Bayesian estimate, so it comes with a posterior: a covariance for all the ratings at once. Each simulated season first draws its own "true" ratings from that posterior (a team's rating SD is about ±3.8 points in the NFL and ±5 in college right now), then plays every game with those. So a team that is secretly good tends to win many of its games in the same simulated season. That within-season correlation is what widens the win distributions and title odds. To avoid double-counting, the per-game noise is shrunk so a single game is exactly as uncertain as before (NFL σ 12.5 → 11.2).

3. The rules

NFL: 17-game standings, then the league's tiebreaker ladder: head-to-head, division record, common games, conference record, strength of victory, strength of schedule, net points in conference games, net points in all games, coin toss. For wild cards with three or more teams, first keep each division's best, then head-to-head sweep only, and so on; whenever a step eliminates a team, the rest restart at step 1. We skip the "combined ranking in points scored and allowed" and "net touchdowns" steps because we simulate margins, not scores. Seeded from the real results, this reproduces all 48 real conference playoff fields, byes and wild-card pairings, 2002–2025. Then the 14-team bracket: #1 seed bye, 2v7/3v6/4v5, reseeding (the #1 seed hosts the lowest survivor), Super Bowl at a neutral site.

College: conference records from conference games; the top two meet in the title game (the Sun Belt still uses its East and West winners). The tiebreak is an approximation: head-to-head, then record against common conference opponents, then our rating (standing in for the analytics composites the SEC, ACC and Big 12 actually use), then a coin. The CFP uses the 2026 rules (confirmed against the CFP's January 2026 announcement): 12 teams, automatic bids for the Big Ten, SEC, ACC and Big 12 champions plus the highest-ranked Group of Six team (champion or not), seven at-large, seeded straight by ranking, top four get byes, first round on campus. Backtests use each season's own format (4 teams through 2023; 2024's "top four champions get byes"; 2025's straight seeding with five champions).

4. The committee is not a formula

We rank teams with a committee proxy: end-of-season rating − 8 points per loss + 4 for a conference title − 12 for Group of Six membership. The weights were fitted to the committee's real final top 25 in 2021–2025. Rating alone orders 94.0% of pairs the way the committee did; adding losses and titles gives 97.2%; the Group of Six term 98.3%, and each step improved every season when that season was held out. It picks the real field exactly in 2024 (12/12) and 10 of 12 in 2025. Without the Group of Six term it had 2025 James Madison 9th (the committee: 24th). It is still a proxy: it knows nothing about injuries, "eye test" or arguments made in the room.

5. The most-likely bracket

A single most-likely full bracket barely exists (each exact outcome is rare). We show a consensus: the NFL's most likely division winners plus the three most likely wild cards, and college's 12 teams most likely to make the CFP, each ordered by expected seed, with the probability next to every slot and the alternatives underneath.

Calibration: when it says 80%, does it happen 80% of the time?

point ratings   ratings drawn (production). Dots on the diagonal are perfectly calibrated; dot size shows how many team-weeks fall in the bin.

The point-rating version is overconfident at both ends: NFL teams it gave 85–95% made the playoffs 77% of the time, and 3% of teams it gave under 5% got in anyway. With drawn ratings those become 83% and 0%. 80% win intervals cover 84% of NFL outcomes (76% without draws) and 90% in college (85%); discrete win totals make an inclusive 10th–90th percentile interval cover a bit more than 80% by construction.

What we learned

Rating uncertainty matters, mostly for spreads. It gives clearly better win distributions in every sample (RPS CIs clear of zero) and fixes the overconfident tails. On single events the gain is small and its CI touches zero in the NFL. On the champion alone it is mixed: point ratings scored slightly better in NFL 2010–17 and college, but that rests on only 24 and 12 season-weeks.
The "hot hand" is the same thing twice. Refitting ratings on each path's simulated results (the way 538 lets Elo react inside a simulation) ties drawing ratings up front: both are ways of making a team's games correlated within one season. Doing both double-counts it, which made the odds too wide (NFL 2010–17 worse by +0.0077 [+0.0012, +0.0142]; 92% of outcomes inside 80% intervals). Production keeps the draw: it is cheaper and keeps single-game calibration by construction.
Records lie early. Extrapolating the current record is worse than flipping coins (+0.0450 [+0.0195, +0.0731] in the NFL). A 3–0 team is not an .800 team.
The market knows more. A simulator fed each remaining game's closing spread beats ours by a wide margin. That comparison cheats (a week-15 line is not known in week 3; it contains later injuries and form), so it is an upper bound, not a fair baseline. Preseason market win totals would be the fair market benchmark, but they are not in the public nflverse data, so that comparison was not run.

Caveats

  • No injuries, quarterback changes, coaching changes, or rest. Ratings change within a simulated season only through the up-front draw.
  • College tiebreakers are approximate, and the CFP committee is a fitted proxy. Bowls are not simulated.
  • Division movers (North Dakota State and Sacramento State joined FBS conferences in 2026) are rated on one scale across their FCS history; their ratings rest on few FBS-level games.
  • The simulation stops once the postseason starts; it would otherwise re-simulate playoff games already played.
  • The backtest is small where it matters most: 8 NFL seasons and 4 college seasons, so champion-odds calibration cannot be pinned down.
  • This is an experiment running in shadow. Nothing else on the site uses it.

Q&A

Why is the favourite's title chance only ~19%?

Because a single-elimination bracket is brutal: even a team that wins each playoff game 65% of the time wins four in a row about 18% of the time, and it first has to get there. Most "title favourite" talk ignores this.

Why use the margin ridge and not the drive simulator?

They carry the same information (their margins correlate 0.96), the ridge is thousands of times cheaper, and the ridge is at least as good on winners. The drive sim's only measured advantage is the exact shape of the margin distribution, which barely matters for who wins a season.

Does a 60% playoff chance mean the model "picked" them?

No. It means that in 6 of 10 simulated seasons they got in. Across many 60% teams, about 6 in 10 should get in; the calibration charts check exactly that.

Why can a team with a better record have lower odds?

Ratings come from margins over two seasons, not just wins, and the remaining schedule matters. Early in a season a lucky 3–0 and a strong 2–1 are hard to tell apart; the simulator weights both what happened and how.

How long does it take?

10,000 seasons take about 0.6 seconds for the NFL and 3 seconds for college (plus ~25 seconds fetching the schedule), well inside the weekly job's limits.
